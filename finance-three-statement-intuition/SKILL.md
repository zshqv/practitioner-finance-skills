# Three-Statement Intuition

**Important**: This skill teaches financial statement intuition for learning purposes. It deliberately uses analogies and simplifications to build understanding — always verify technical entries against accounting standards for professional use.

This skill exists because every tutorial shows you how to fill in a financial statement. None of them explain why the numbers move the way they do. Without the why, you make the same errors repeatedly — adding depreciation when you should subtract it, failing to see why net income and cash are different things, losing the thread when a concept moves from one statement to another.

This skill fixes that. Logic first. Mechanics follow naturally.

---

## The One Thing to Understand Before Anything Else

The three statements are not three separate documents. They are three different ways of looking at the same business reality.

The P&L is the story. It tells you what happened — how much the business earned and spent over a period of time.

The Balance Sheet is the photograph. It freezes the business at one moment in time and shows you everything it owns and everything it owes.

The Cash Flow Statement is the bank account. It proves the story is real by showing you actual cash moving in and out.

If the story says the business made money but the bank account is empty — something is wrong. The cash flow statement is what catches that.

---

## The P&L — The Story of a Period

The P&L asks one question: did this business create value during this period?

Value created = Revenue earned minus everything it cost to earn that revenue.

An expense on the P&L does not mean cash left the building right now. It means the business used up something of value to generate revenue.

When a company pays salaries — cash leaves and an expense is recorded. Simple.

When a company records depreciation — no cash leaves. The machine was paid for years ago. What is happening now is the business is admitting: that machine is worth less today than it was last year, and we used it to generate revenue, so we should recognise that cost.

The P&L records the economic reality. The cash flow statement records what actually hit the bank account. They are deliberately different — and that difference is meaningful.

Net Income is the P&L output. It flows into both the Balance Sheet and the Cash Flow Statement. This is the first link between statements.

## The Balance Sheet — The Photograph at a Moment in Time

The Balance Sheet asks: what does this business own, and who has a claim on it?

Everything a business owns was paid for by someone. Either by lenders (liabilities) or by the owners (equity).

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

This is not an accounting trick. It is a logical truth. Every asset came from somewhere. That somewhere is always either debt or equity.

At the end of every period, net income flows into the Balance Sheet through Retained Earnings — which sits inside Equity. If the business made profit and paid no dividends, equity grows. The balance sheet must still balance — so assets also increase.

Common entry errors and why they happen:

Adding depreciation to assets instead of subtracting: Depreciation reduces asset value. A machine bought for 100 that depreciates 10 per year is worth 90 after year one. The asset goes down. The confusion happens because on the Cash Flow Statement, depreciation is added back — same word, opposite direction, different reason.

Confusing timing of revenue and cash: Revenue is recorded when earned, not when cash is received. If you deliver a service in March but the client pays in April — March P&L shows revenue, March cash flow shows nothing, March balance sheet shows a receivable. The receivable is the bridge.

## The Cash Flow Statement — The Bank Account That Proves the Story

The Cash Flow Statement asks: where did cash actually come from, and where did it actually go?

Three sections:
- Operating Activities: cash generated by running the business
- Investing Activities: cash spent on or received from assets
- Financing Activities: cash from or returned to lenders and shareholders

Why depreciation is added back — the most confusing entry for self-taught learners:

The P&L subtracted depreciation as an expense. This reduced net income. But did cash actually leave this year? No. The cash left when the machine was purchased years ago. Depreciation is the annual recognition of that historical cost — a non-cash accounting entry.

So the Cash Flow Statement says: the P&L deducted depreciation, but no cash actually moved this year — so add it back to get to real cash.

Net Income + Depreciation (add back) +/- Working Capital changes = Cash from Operations

Working capital changes: If receivables increase — P&L counted it as revenue but cash has not arrived. Cash flow subtracts it. If payables increase — P&L counted it as expense but business has not paid. Cash flow adds it back. This is why profitable companies sometimes run out of cash.

The ending cash on the Cash Flow Statement must equal the cash balance on the Balance Sheet. This is the final proof that all three statements tell the same story from different angles.

## Mentor Checklist — Before You Build Any Model

Before touching a spreadsheet, answer these without looking anything up:

- What does net income represent and why is it different from cash?
- Where does net income go after the P&L is done?
- Why does the balance sheet always balance — not as a rule, but as a logical truth?
- Why is depreciation added back in the cash flow statement?
- If receivables increase, does cash go up or down and why?
- If payables increase, does cash go up or down and why?
- What does a company look like if it has high profit but negative operating cash flow?
- What is the difference between capex and an operating expense?

## Common Confusions Answered in Plain Language

Why does depreciation appear in three places? It affects three different things. It reduces profit on the P&L. It reduces asset value on the Balance Sheet. It gets added back on the Cash Flow to reconcile profit to cash. Same economic fact, three different reflections.

Why can a profitable company go bankrupt? Because profit is not cash. A company can show net income and still have zero in the bank if clients have not paid and debt repayments are due. The cash flow statement shows this. The P&L hides it.

Why does equity increase when the company makes profit? Because profit belongs to the owners. When the business earns money and does not distribute it, it accumulates as retained earnings inside equity.

What is EBITDA and why does everyone use it? Earnings Before Interest Tax Depreciation and Amortisation. It approximates operating cash generation before financing decisions and accounting conventions. Used because it strips out things that vary by capital structure and accounting policy — making businesses easier to compare. Never confuse it with actual cash flow.
